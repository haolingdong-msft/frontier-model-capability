Evaluating frontier models across capability, cost, response time, and task fit

A multi-dimensional analysis of 11 frontier model families across five reasoning effort tiers, four cost-capability benchmarks, end-to-end latency, and 17 native task evaluations.

Why choosing the right model for complex tasks is hard

When developers evaluate AI coding agents on a short prompt, model choice feels straightforward: pick the highest-ranked model on a public leaderboard and move on. On complex, long-running engineering workflows—such as multi-file code migrations, repository-wide API reviews, full-stack UI generation, or deep debugging loops—that intuition breaks down.

A model is not a single operating point

Modern frontier models expose up to five reasoning effort tiers: Low (1), Medium (2), High (3), Xhigh (4), and Max (5). Raising reasoning effort within the same model family can lift benchmark capability by 14 to 29 points, while multiplying per-task cost by 4x to 19x and stretching single-turn latency from 7 seconds to nearly 12 minutes. Comparing one model at Low effort against another at Max effort measures the effort setting rather than the model family.

Single-number leaderboards obscure engineering trade-offs

A model configuration that tops an aggregate intelligence index may cost $10 per task and take 11 minutes per turn, while a peer configuration reaches 90% of that score for $0.59 in under a minute—or outperforms the aggregate leader on specific domains like frontend design, code migration, or structured Word document generation.

Long-running workflows amplify small model mismatches

In a multi-turn agentic workflow that invokes dozens of tool calls and subagent turns, per-turn latency and token cost compound rapidly. Choosing a model without understanding its capability-to-cost curve, end-to-end response time, and task specialization routinely turns a 3-minute, sub-dollar job into a multi-hour, high-cost run.

To make model selection concrete and reproducible, we analyzed 11 frontier model families across 5 reasoning effort tiers and 17 native task benchmarks (274 captured configuration rows). Every chart below preserves the exact published benchmark units without cross-benchmark normalization or synthetic composites.

GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6.1 Terra define the upper-left efficiency frontier

On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5.0, GPT-6.1 Sol climbs from 34.5 at Low ($0.16/task) to 46.1 at Medium ($0.36), 50.2 at High ($0.59), and 54.5 at Max ($1.27). At High effort, GPT-6.1 Sol delivers nearly 90% of the highest score on the leaderboard for roughly 6% to 18% of the per-task cost of flagship models. One tier lower in price, GPT-6.1 Terra reaches 42.3 at High for $0.26 and 51.0 at Max for $0.96, keeping even its maximum reasoning tier under a dollar per task.

Flagship models reach the highest ceiling but hit a steep cost inflection above High

Claude Opus 5.5 achieves the highest published v5.0 capability score (58.4 at Max), followed by GPT-6 Astra (56.9 at Max). However, moving Claude Opus 5.5 from High (51.7 at $3.46) to Max (58.4 at $10.04) nearly triples the cost for a +6.7 point gain. Moving Claude Sonnet 5.5 from High (41.1 at $1.64) to Max (54.2 at $10.02) multiplies per-task cost by 6.1x, making Sonnet Max just as expensive per task as Opus Max despite a lower capability ceiling.

Higher reasoning effort is not always monotonically better

Switching the benchmark selector to CursorBench 4.0 or ARC-AGI-1/2 reveals cases where extra reasoning tokens add cost without improving accuracy—and can even overthink tasks:

  • On CursorBench 4.0, Gemini 4 Argon improves from Low (44.00% at $0.14) to Medium (51.78% at $0.20) to High (58.67% at $0.32), whereas Grok 4.7 drops from 52.89% at Medium ($0.32) to 50.44% at High ($0.45) and 48.00% at Xhigh ($0.65).
  • On ARC-AGI-2, Claude Opus 5.5 peaks at High (93.33% at $0.41/task) and slightly regresses at Xhigh (92.50% at $0.67) and Max (91.67% at $1.85). Meanwhile, GPT-6.1 Sol Max reaches 94.17% for just $0.25/task, outscoring Opus Max at less than one-seventh the cost.

The 7–15 second interactive sweet spot

For interactive coding and rapid subagent tool loops, several configurations deliver strong capability while keeping median turn latency under 15 seconds: GPT-6.1 Terra Medium (7.1s, score 38.1) and High (11.2s, score 42.3), Claude Sonnet 5.5 Medium (7.6s, score 30.3) and High (15.4s, score 41.1), GPT-6.1 Sol Medium (12.1s, score 46.1), and GPT-6 Astra Medium (13.0s, score 51.5). Notably, GPT-6 Astra Medium and GPT-6.1 Sol Medium achieve virtually the same ~12–13s latency as Low effort while jumping +8.5 to +11.6 points in capability.

The Max-effort latency cliff

Stepping up to Max effort pushes median single-turn response time to 83.5s on GPT-6.1 Terra, 275.8s (~4.6 minutes) on GPT-6 Astra, 284.2s (~4.7 minutes) on GPT-6.1 Sol, 458.6s (~7.6 minutes) on Claude Sonnet 5.5, and 705.2s (~11.8 minutes) on Claude Opus 5.5. In a 10-turn serial agent loop, running a model at Max effort adds 45 to 115 minutes of waiting on model inference alone—making High or Medium effort far more practical for iterative implementation steps.
